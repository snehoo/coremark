How Cambridge Checkpoint papers are marked: score, percentage and grading explained

The Checkpoint result is on a 0 to 50 scale, not a percentage. Here is how the marking actually works, what the performance bands mean, and the one question most parents are really asking.

Quick answer

Cambridge Lower Secondary Checkpoint scores are reported on a 0 to 50 scale since May 2023. The score is not a raw percentage — Cambridge converts raw paper marks to a scaled score and reports it alongside strand-level subscores. There is no single pass mark; performance is described in bands (Exceeding, Meeting, Approaching, Below). The most useful number on your child's result sheet is not the total score but the strand subscores, which show exactly which topics need work before Upper Secondary.

Every parent of a Cambridge Lower Secondary student hits the same wall when their child's Checkpoint result arrives. The score is out of 50. There are numbers for each strand. It does not look like a percentage. The school may or may not explain it well. So the searches start: "cambridge checkpoint grading percentage", "what is a good checkpoint score", "cambridge checkpoint score range".

This page explains the complete picture: how Cambridge converts a student's raw paper performance into that 0–50 number, what the performance bands mean, and — most importantly — how to actually use the result to plan what to work on next.

What the Checkpoint score is out of

Since the May 2023 exam series, Cambridge Lower Secondary Checkpoint results in Mathematics (0862), Science (0893) and Computing (0860) are all reported on a scale of 0 to 50. This is a single overall subject score, not a raw paper total.

The old scale is retired

Before May 2023, Cambridge reported Checkpoint results on a 0 to 6 strand-level scale. If you find any website, worksheet, or third-party guide that refers to scores "out of 6" for Cambridge Lower Secondary Checkpoint, it is describing the old system. All results issued from May 2023 onwards use the 0 to 50 scale.

How raw marks become a scaled 0–50 score

A student sitting Cambridge Lower Secondary Maths Checkpoint answers two written papers. Each paper awards raw marks for each question. Cambridge collects those raw marks and converts them to the 0–50 scaled score through a process called statistical moderation.

What scaling means

Scaling adjusts for small variations in difficulty between different exam series. A paper in one year might happen to be slightly harder than the equivalent paper a year earlier. Without scaling, a student who sat a harder paper would be unfairly penalised. The scaled score corrects for this so that a 38 this May means the same level of achievement as a 38 last October.

The practical implication: you cannot reverse-engineer the 0–50 score from raw marks by dividing and multiplying. A student who scores 78 out of 100 raw marks does not necessarily receive a scaled score of 39. The relationship depends on that series' grade boundaries, which Cambridge sets after the exam based on examiner review.

ⓘ
What is publishedCambridge publishes grade boundaries for each series in the School Support Hub (accessible to registered Cambridge schools). The boundaries show the minimum scaled score required for each performance band in that specific series. They are not available in advance.

The four performance bands

Every Cambridge Lower Secondary Checkpoint result includes a performance band descriptor alongside the 0–50 score. The four bands are:

Exceeding
Exceeding the standard — working well above expectations for the stage
Meeting
Meeting the standard — performing as expected for Stage 7, 8 or 9
Approaching
Approaching the standard — some gaps, closer to expected than not
Below
Below the standard — significant gaps relative to stage expectations

Cambridge does not publish fixed score ranges for each band. The cut-scores are determined by the chief examiner and standardisation panel after each exam series, taking into account the specific difficulty of that year's papers. A school can access the series-specific boundaries through the School Support Hub.

What this means for revision planning

Because band boundaries shift each series, chasing a specific raw target score is not as useful as closing the strand-level gaps that the result sheet identifies. A student who improves on their weakest strands will improve their scaled score regardless of where the bands fall.

What percentage do I need? (the real question parents are asking)

The question "what percentage do I need to pass Cambridge Checkpoint?" is the most common search behind this topic, and the answer requires a reframe of how Checkpoint works.

There is no pass or fail. Cambridge Checkpoint is a diagnostic assessment, not a qualifying exam. A student cannot fail Checkpoint in the way they could fail a GCSE or A-level. The result is a snapshot of where they are in the Cambridge Lower Secondary programme, used by schools and parents to understand which areas need more work before progressing to Upper Secondary.

There is no single percentage target. Because the score is on a 0–50 scale and the band boundaries shift each series, there is no equivalent of "you need 65% to pass". What matters is the performance band and, more specifically, the strand subscores.

"Checkpoint is not the thing your child passes or fails. It is the report that tells you what to work on before Upper Secondary." Snehal Patel, CoreMark

That said, schools and parents often use Checkpoint scores to make practical decisions about readiness for Cambridge IGCSE. A student Meeting or Exceeding the standard consistently across strands is generally considered well-prepared. A student Approaching or Below should use the result to identify which specific strand to target, not to convert it to a percentage and compare it to an arbitrary threshold.

Strand-level scores and why they matter more

The 0–50 overall score is the headline number, but the most useful information in a Checkpoint result is the strand-level subscores printed alongside it.

Each Cambridge Lower Secondary subject is divided into curriculum strands. Mathematics 0862 has strands including Number, Algebra, Geometry, Measure, Statistics and Probability. Science 0893 has strands across Biology, Chemistry and Physics. Computing 0860 covers Computing concepts, Digital communication, Data handling and Computer systems.

What the overall score tells you What strand subscores tell you
Which performance band the student is in overall Which specific curriculum strands are strong and which are weak
Useful for comparing across subjects Directly actionable: tells you which topic to revise next
Hides variance (a strong algebra score can mask a weak geometry score) Shows variance: two students with the same total can have completely different strand profiles
Does not indicate where to focus revision effort Is the starting point for any targeted revision plan

A student who scores 36 overall in Mathematics but has a low Algebra subscore should spend revision time on Algebra specifically, not on general maths practice. The overall score gets them into the Meeting band. The strand score tells them what is holding them back within that band.

✅
How to read the result sheetAsk the school for the Statement of Results, which breaks down the score by strand. If the school only provides a total score, ask specifically for the strand-level breakdown — Cambridge provides it and it should be passed on to parents.

How individual marks are awarded on the papers

On the actual exam papers, marks are awarded using a structured mark scheme. Understanding the mark types helps when reviewing your child's practice work.

Code Name Awarded when
M1 Method mark The student shows correct working steps, even if the final answer is wrong. This is the mark that "show your working" protects.
A1 Accuracy mark The correct final answer. Usually only given when the method is also correct — M1 is often required first.
B1 Independent mark A specific correct statement, awarded regardless of whether surrounding working is right or wrong.
FT / ECF Follow Through / Error Carried Forward If a student makes an error in one step but continues with correct method, they can still earn marks for later steps. A child who shows full working is protected even if they make one arithmetic slip.

The practical takeaway for parents: a student who leaves blanks or only writes final answers loses method marks on every question they struggle with. A student who writes out their working — even when unsure — can often earn partial credit. This habit is worth reinforcing before any timed practice.

Why showing working matters

On a typical Checkpoint Maths question worth 3 marks, the breakdown might be M1 (correct method) + M1 (correct intermediate step) + A1 (correct final answer). A student who works through the method correctly but makes a final arithmetic error can still earn 2 out of 3. A student who just writes a wrong number earns 0 out of 3.

Frequently asked questions

Since May 2023, Cambridge Lower Secondary Checkpoint scores are reported on a scale of 0 to 50. This replaced the old 0 to 6 scale. The score is not a raw mark — it is a scaled score derived from the total raw marks across all papers in a subject, then reported alongside strand-level subscores.

There is no single pass percentage for Cambridge Checkpoint. The assessment is diagnostic rather than pass/fail. Scores are described using performance bands — Exceeding, Meeting, Approaching, or Below the standard — and Cambridge does not publish a fixed cut-score for these bands in advance. The grade boundaries are set after each exam series based on that year's papers. Parents should read the Statement of Results to see which band their child achieved and the strand-level subscores that show where specific gaps lie.

There is no universally agreed target since Checkpoint is diagnostic, not competitive. A score that places a student in the Meeting or Exceeding band shows they are on track for Cambridge Upper Secondary. The more useful measure for a parent is the strand-level subscores, which pinpoint the specific curriculum areas where revision would make the biggest difference.

Before May 2023, Cambridge Lower Secondary Checkpoint results were reported on a scale of 0 to 6 for each discipline. From May 2023 onwards, Cambridge moved to a 0 to 50 scale for the overall subject score, with strand-level subscores also reported. Any revision material or score table using the 0 to 6 scale is out of date.

Checkpoint is not a formal entry requirement for Cambridge IGCSE — schools set their own internal progression policies. However, many Cambridge schools use Checkpoint results as one factor when advising students on IGCSE subject choices and sets. A strong Checkpoint result in a subject is a useful signal that the student is ready to handle its IGCSE equivalent. A weak result in a specific strand is a useful flag to address before Upper Secondary begins.
