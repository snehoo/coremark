Is IGCSE Lower Secondary the same as Cambridge Lower Secondary? No — here's why.

"IGCSE Lower Secondary" isn't a real programme name — it's what parents type when searching for how the two connect. Here is what each programme actually is, how they sit in the Cambridge Pathway, and exactly what changes when a student moves from Stage 9 Checkpoint to their first IGCSE subjects.

Quick answer

Cambridge Lower Secondary (Stages 7–9, ages 11–14) and Cambridge IGCSE (ages 14–16) are separate programmes. Lower Secondary is a foundation curriculum; IGCSE is the internationally recognised qualification that follows it. The optional Cambridge Checkpoint exam, taken at the end of Stage 9, bridges the two — schools use it to advise students on IGCSE subject choices and readiness, but it is not a formal entry requirement.

The phrase "IGCSE lower secondary" shows up constantly in searches, suggesting many parents assume the two are the same programme or that Lower Secondary is some kind of junior IGCSE. It is not. They sit at different stages of the Cambridge Pathway, serve different purposes, and have meaningfully different assessment structures.

This guide explains exactly what each programme is, how they differ, and what the Cambridge Lower Secondary Checkpoint result means for a student's IGCSE journey.

  • 02Cambridge IGCSE is the Upper Secondary qualification for ages 14–16. Results appear on university applications and employer CVs.

The Cambridge Pathway: where each programme sits

Cambridge Assessment International Education organises its programmes into a continuous ladder called the Cambridge Pathway. Lower Secondary and IGCSE are two rungs of that ladder, not two names for the same thing.

Programme Typical ages Stages / Years Purpose
Cambridge Primary 5–11 Stages 1–6 Foundation literacy and numeracy
Cambridge Lower Secondary 11–14 Stages 7–9 Building subject knowledge before qualifications
Cambridge IGCSE 14–16 Years 10–11 Internationally recognised qualification
Cambridge AS & A Level 16–19 Lower & Upper Sixth Pre-university qualification

A student who joins a Cambridge school at age 11 typically enters Stage 7 of Cambridge Lower Secondary. By age 14, they complete Stage 9 and may sit the Cambridge Checkpoint. They then begin IGCSE, usually in a narrower selection of subjects, and sit those exams at age 16.

What Cambridge Lower Secondary actually is

Cambridge Lower Secondary is a curriculum framework, not a qualification. It defines what students should know and be able to do across Stages 7, 8 and 9 in core subjects: Maths (syllabus 0862), Science (0893), Computing (0860), English (0851), and others.

Lower Secondary

Cambridge Lower Secondary

  • Stages 7, 8 and 9 (ages 11–14)
  • Curriculum framework with learning objectives
  • Optional Checkpoint assessment at Stage 9
  • Checkpoint result: diagnostic (0–50 scale, six performance bands)
  • Not a formal qualification — no grades on a certificate for UCAS
  • Prepares for IGCSE subjects

Upper Secondary

Cambridge IGCSE

  • Years 10–11 (ages 14–16)
  • Formal subject qualifications
  • Mandatory examinations in each subject
  • Results: graded A*–G (or 9–1 in some markets)
  • Internationally recognised — appears on university applications
  • Requires IGCSE-level preparation per subject

The key distinction: Lower Secondary does not result in a qualification. The Cambridge Checkpoint result is a diagnostic report card, not a certificate that can be submitted to universities. What matters at the end of IGCSE is the IGCSE result. What Lower Secondary does is give students the subject knowledge and study habits they need to succeed in those IGCSE years.

What "diagnostic" means

A diagnostic assessment is designed to identify where a student is, not to issue a pass or fail. The Cambridge Checkpoint is diagnostic in the same way that a health check is diagnostic — it tells you what is working well and what needs attention, not whether you passed or failed being healthy.

What Cambridge IGCSE actually is

Cambridge IGCSE (International General Certificate of Secondary Education) is a genuine qualification. Results are graded on an A* to G scale and are recognised by universities, employers and governments in over 160 countries. Students typically study 7–10 IGCSE subjects.

IGCSE is examined primarily through written papers at the end of Year 11, though some subjects include coursework components. The difficulty and volume of content is substantially greater than Cambridge Lower Secondary. A student entering Year 10 IGCSE Maths, for example, will encounter topics that build directly on their Stage 9 Maths foundation — but with greater depth, harder questions, and formal exam conditions that count toward their final grade.

How Checkpoint connects to IGCSE

The Cambridge Lower Secondary Checkpoint is the assessment that sits at the junction between the two programmes. It is taken at the end of Stage 9 (typically age 13–14) and covers Maths, Science and English. Computing can also be assessed.

Checkpoint is optional — schools choose whether to enter students, and IGCSE does not formally require it. But it serves several practical functions in the transition:

  • Subject readiness signal: A strong Checkpoint result in Maths indicates the student is ready to handle IGCSE Maths. A weak strand score in, say, Algebra is a flag to address before Year 10 starts.
  • Set and stream advice: Many Cambridge schools use Checkpoint results when advising students on which IGCSE sets (foundation vs extended tier) or which optional subjects to take.
  • Curriculum alignment check: Cambridge Lower Secondary learning objectives are deliberately mapped to IGCSE entry-level expectations. Checkpoint measures whether a student has met those expectations.
💡
What the strand scores are forThe most useful part of a Checkpoint result for IGCSE planning is not the total score but the strand-level subscores. A student with a low Algebra subscore in Maths Checkpoint should target Algebra specifically before IGCSE Maths revision begins — because IGCSE Algebra is harder, faster, and examined under more pressure.

Importantly, Checkpoint does not determine whether a student can sit IGCSE subjects. No Cambridge school policy requires a specific Checkpoint score to progress to IGCSE. What it does is give the school and family better information to make sensible subject and streaming choices.

What changes when students move up to IGCSE

Parents sometimes underestimate how different IGCSE is from Lower Secondary. A few specific things change:

What is genuinely harder at IGCSE

Content depth increases significantly. A Stage 9 student studying equations works with linear expressions; IGCSE Maths introduces simultaneous equations, quadratics, and function notation in the first year. Science becomes more mathematically demanding. Computing requires structured programming, not just understanding of concepts. The pace is faster and the exams are higher-stakes.

  • Narrower subject set, deeper study: At IGCSE, students typically drop 5–8 subjects and go much deeper in the 7–10 they keep. The breadth of Lower Secondary gives way to depth.
  • Grades now count: IGCSE results appear on the student's certificate and follow them. Performance bands from Checkpoint do not. The stakes are genuinely higher.
  • Exam technique matters more: At IGCSE, how a student answers — showing method, using correct notation, structuring an extended response — directly affects marks in a way that is more consequential than in Checkpoint.
  • Independent study becomes important: IGCSE content volume typically requires students to revise outside of class in a structured way for the first time.
