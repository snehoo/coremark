What the four weeks look like

Each week has one focus, one Coach mode, one paper task and one thing to review afterwards. The order matters more than the exact days. Weak topics come first, then teaching, then retesting, then a full mock.

A 25-minute session, minute by minute (a suggestion)

This outline is a suggestion, not a rule. The Coach page says that twenty to thirty minutes on most days works better than one long weekend session, and a 25-minute slot is a sensible size for a learn-mode loop plus a short check.

  1. 0 to 2 minutes. Open a fresh chat in the Coach project, type "learn" and the topic, and start your own timer. A new chat each session keeps the history easy to scroll back through.
  2. 2 to 4 minutes. Coach opens with a map of the sub-concepts in the topic. Pick the one that matches what was on the last worksheet.
  3. 4 to 12 minutes. Work through one sub-concept: the diagram where one helps, an everyday example, the exam wording, and the misconception most students have.
  4. 12 to 17 minutes. Teach it back in your child's own words, then answer one apply question on paper and type the final answer.
  5. 17 to 21 minutes. Do the three-question mini-check. No marks are given, so the point is to notice what is still fuzzy.
  6. 21 to 24 minutes. Write two lines in the notebook: the misconception that caught you out, and the question you want to see again.
  7. 24 to 25 minutes. Stop. Do not start another sub-concept. The next session picks up from the notebook.

How do I pick the weak topics in week one?

Use two sources, and let the paper settle any disagreement. The first is what Coach brings back. Coach tracks weak topics and offers a quick review of them at the start of later sessions, so note which topics keep returning. The second is the end-of-exam report from any topic test, which is grouped by idea rather than by question number.

From those two lists, choose two or three topics that meet both tests. First, the idea group lost the most marks in the report. Second, the topic is used by later topics, so a gap here hurts every topic after it. A topic that is only a little shaky can wait.

  • Two or three topics, not seven
  • Each one has a clear idea group in the report that lost marks
  • Each one is needed for later topics in the year
  • Your child has been told which topic comes first and why

What do I do when a week goes wrong?

Most weeks will go wrong somewhere, usually because of a school test, an illness or a session that simply did not happen. Keep the sequence and move the calendar, rather than doubling up to catch up. The plan is designed to be flexible, and a missed day should not turn into a missed topic.

If your child says a paper is too hard, Coach is built to shrink it. Its own guidance has it move to a half-length paper or switch to teaching, on the principle that kindness beats coverage. That is a good moment to pause, not to push through. If a whole week is lost, carry the weakest topic into the next week and drop the newest topic from the plan.

⚠️
Keep the mock. If time runs short, shorten the week 3 paper before you skip the week 4 mock, because the mock is the only piece that shows exam timing.

How does exam date mode change the plan?

Exam date mode is a Coach feature you switch on by telling it the exam date. It then focuses on the weakest and highest-value topics first, which means the plan above should be re-ordered rather than followed line by line. Tell Coach what your child is studying this week too, because Coach does not follow your school's chapter order.

Exam date mode changes the priorities. It does not promise that a particular topic will appear on the paper, and nobody outside the exam board can tell you that.

Where do handwriting and timed paper practice fit?

Coach's exam mode asks for working on every question worth two or more marks, so handwriting is already part of the plan. Coach asks for photos only of written work or drawings, and only when it needs them. Paper practice is where the timing skill comes from, and that is what the CoreMark PDF boosters are for: fixed 20-question booster PDFs on a single topic, printed and done on paper.

Use your own timer in every paper, because Coach gives a time target but does not stop the clock. Expect no hints during a mock. Practice marks from Coach or a booster are not Cambridge Checkpoint scores, and the way Checkpoint results are scaled is explained in the Checkpoint grading system guide. Where to find official past papers is covered in this past papers guide.

What should we stop doing?

Stop re-reading notes as the main revision method. Re-reading feels productive because the material looks familiar, but familiarity is not recall. In a 2013 review of ten study techniques, the authors rated practice testing and spaced practice as high utility, and rereading as low utility, while noting that the evidence varies by learner, material and task (Dunlosky and colleagues, 2013).

A 2006 lab study of prose passages found that students who took recall tests remembered more a week later than students who restudied, even though the restudy group felt more confident (Roediger and Karpicke, 2006). Those were reading experiments, so treat them as a reason to test, not as proof of what happens in your child's maths class.

Replace the re-reading time with the teach-it-back loop and the short topic tests. Stop before the notes become the revision.
