Diagrams are where parents trust AI least, and they are right to be careful. A wrong number in a sentence is easy to spot. A wrong angle in a neat drawing can look perfectly convincing to a 12 year old who is still learning what an obtuse angle looks like.

This guide gives you a set of questions to ask of any AI-drawn diagram, whether it comes from CoreMark Coach or another tool. It also explains what Coach is designed to do, so you know what to expect.

Does every number and label match the question?

Start here, before you look at the shape. Read the question text, then look at the diagram and list every number, angle, unit and letter in the picture. Each one should appear in the question, and each value the question gives should appear in the picture.

A diagram that shows 56 degrees when the question says 65 is wrong, however tidy it looks. The same goes for a missing side length the question needs, or a point labelled B where the question asks about A. Angle questions are where this bites most; the post on why geometry and angle problems get harder covers the topic itself.

⚠️
Quick test. Cover the question and ask your child what the picture says. If the answer does not match the words, the diagram needs checking.

Does the shape look like the numbers say?

Check the geometry against the values before anything else. An obtuse angle should look obtuse, which means wider than a right angle. A 30 degree angle should look small, and a right angle should carry a small square.

Sides are the same test. If two lines are marked equal, they should look equal, and if a triangle is labelled as having one long side, the drawing should show it. Parallel lines should be marked as parallel, usually with matching arrowheads.

Is anything labelled or coloured that the question does not need?

Extra labels are a problem because they can give away the method, or lead a child to use a value the question never asked for. A good diagram labels only what the question needs.

Colour should mean something. In a force diagram, one colour might separate the forces acting on an object from the resultant. If colours are only decorative, or there are more than three colours plus black, ask why they are there. Coach is built to use no more than three colours plus black, and only where colour carries meaning.

When should a diagram say NOT TO SCALE?

A diagram should say NOT TO SCALE whenever its lengths or angles are not drawn to the stated values. If the picture is a sketch and no note appears, the proportions may be misleading, so rely on the numbers in the question, not on how the drawing looks.

Coach is built to add that note when values are not to scale. Its absence is a reason to check, not proof of an error.

What should you verify for each common diagram type?

Each type of diagram has its own usual failure. Use this table as a quick guide when you sit down with your child.

Diagram typeWhat to verify
Angles in parallel linesThe parallel lines are marked as parallel, the given angle has the value in the question, and the angle you are asked to find sits in the right place relative to the transversal.
A circuitEach component is the one the question names, the cell and bulbs are connected in the stated way (series or parallel), and any ammeter or voltmeter is placed where the question puts it.
A plant or animal cellEvery labelled part is a part the question asks about, each label points to the right structure, and the cell type (plant or animal) matches the question.
A line graphAxis labels and units match the question, the scale is even, each point sits at the value stated, and the line joins the points the question describes.
A flowchartEvery decision has a yes path and a no path, the arrows follow the order the question describes, and the start and end boxes are both present.
A number lineThe end values match the question, the marks are evenly spaced for the scale used, and any open or closed circles match the inequality.

What is CoreMark Coach built to do with a diagram?

As of 20 October 2026, CoreMark Coach is built to follow a fixed set of diagram rules. The diagram is drawn before the question text appears, so the picture is part of the question rather than an afterthought. It labels only what the question needs and keeps the drawing flat and uncluttered.

Coach also runs a consistency check: every number and label in the drawing should match the question text, and the geometry should visibly match the values, such as an obtuse angle looking obtuse. Where values are not to scale, it adds a NOT TO SCALE note.

When a child draws a diagram on paper and uploads a photo, Coach is built to draw the correct version and compare the two, naming the specific differences. If graphics are not available, it falls back to a text drawing for simple cases such as number lines, flowcharts and truth tables, or it swaps the question for one that needs no diagram.

Diagrams only appear when the Claude setting Code execution and file creation is switched on. You can find it under Settings, then Capabilities. According to Anthropic's help centre, this setting is available on all plans, including Free.

What checklist should you screenshot before trusting a diagram?

Save this list on your phone and run through it each time a diagram appears in a session.

  • Every number in the picture appears in the question, and every value the question gives appears in the picture.
  • The angles and sides look like their stated values, so an obtuse angle looks obtuse.
  • Only the labels the question needs are shown.
  • Colours separate things that matter, and there are no more than three colours plus black.
  • A NOT TO SCALE note is present wherever the drawing is not to scale.
  • The diagram type matches the table above, and its key features are all there.
  • If anything looks off, ask Coach to show its working or redraw the diagram.

How do you get a wrong diagram corrected?

Ask Coach to redraw the diagram and say exactly what looks wrong, such as the angle value or a missing label. Then ask it to show its working so you can see how the values feed into the answer. Coach is built to say so and replace a question if it spots its own mistake.

Check against a second source when the diagram matters for a mark. Force diagrams carry the same risk, because a wrong arrow changes the answer; for the topic itself, see why forces, energy and motion calculations trip kids up. A CoreMark booster PDF prints its own diagrams, and for a fixed paper question a printed diagram in a booster or a textbook can be the better reference. If the two disagree, treat the printed one as your first reference, since it was drawn once and checked, and ask Coach to explain the difference. Printed diagrams can contain errors too, so the textbook or your child's teacher has the last word.

⚠️
Do not argue with the answer alone. Ask the question again on paper and check the drawing, rather than telling Coach that its diagram is simply wrong.

Where do AI diagrams still go wrong?

This section describes general failure modes, not specific examples. CoreMark has no screenshots or recorded examples of Coach's diagram output to show here, so please do not read this as a catalogue of observed errors.

In general, AI drawings can fail in a few ways. A drawing can look tidy while one value is wrong. A shape can be drawn to the right proportions while its labels point to the wrong part. A chart can have a scale that looks even but skips values. A flowchart can have arrows in an order that reads well but does not follow the question. These errors are easy to miss when the picture looks confident, which is why the checks above come before the look of the drawing.
