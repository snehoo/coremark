AI tutor or human tutor in India? How to choose for a Cambridge Lower Secondary child

A good tutor notices things software cannot. An AI tutor is there at nine at night. Plenty of families do best with a mix.

Quick answer

A human tutor adapts to your child's mood, keeps them accountable and can see handwriting and body language. An AI tutor such as CoreMark Coach is available any hour, never tires and marks every practice paper. Many Cambridge Lower Secondary families use a tutor for motivation and an AI tutor between sessions. Which to choose depends on what is actually holding your child back.

CoreMark makes CoreMark Coach, an AI tutor, so we have an interest in this question. We have tried to write it the way we would want a rival to write it. We do not give an average tutor fee, because we do not have a source for one.

Indian families are the best-placed people to judge this, because you already know what tutoring costs in your city and which tutors are worth it. What follows gives you a way to compare, not a verdict. We start with what a person does that software cannot, since that is where AI claims tend to get inflated.

What does a good human tutor do that an AI tutor cannot?

A human tutor reads the child. They notice when a student goes quiet, when a confident answer is a guess, or when a bad day at school has followed them to the desk. They also create accountability: a child who has a tutor arriving at five is more likely to have tried the homework than one who has to open an app.

A tutor can watch handwriting form on the page, point at a step, and hear the way a child pronounces a word. They know your school, your child's teacher and the chapter you reached last week. They can also tell you honestly that your child is not working. None of this is a criticism of Coach. It is a list of things we have not built and would not claim.

What does an AI tutor do better?

It is there when the tutor is not. CoreMark Coach is available at any hour, so a question that comes up at nine on a Sunday night gets an answer instead of waiting until Thursday. It is patient with repetition, since asking for the same idea a fourth time carries no social cost for the child. It sets fresh questions each session, as many as your Claude plan allows, where a printed booster stops at twenty. And it marks every practice paper the same way, showing where method marks and accuracy marks were earned and the first wrong step.

Those are real strengths for practice and explanation. They are strengths of availability and consistency, and they leave out motivation and judgement.

What does a mixed week look like?

The most common pattern we would suggest is a tutor for the human parts and Coach for the volume. A sample week for a Stage 9 student might be:

  • Monday: a short Coach Learn session on the topic from school that day.
  • Wednesday: tutor session, bringing the questions Coach flagged as weak.
  • Friday: a Coach topic test, with the report shown to the tutor.
  • Weekend: a printed paper from booster practice, marked with the worked solutions.

That arrangement uses the tutor's hour for judgement rather than for marking, which is where many families feel an hour is wasted.

How do you hand Coach's report to a human tutor?

Copy the end-of-paper report from Coach and give it to the tutor as the agenda for the next session. Coach's report is grouped by idea rather than question number, so a line such as "multi-step algebra: arithmetic slip in step 3" is something a tutor can act on in minutes. It also names the first wrong step in each question, which saves the tutor from re-marking the paper to find where it went wrong.

Tell the tutor that the report comes from an AI and can be wrong. A good tutor will check one or two items against the child's actual working, which is also a useful habit for you. If the tutor disagrees with something Coach said, that is information worth having, not a failure of either.

How can you tell whether your child needs a person?

Look at why your child is stuck. If the problem is understanding or practice volume, an AI tutor can help. If the problem is confidence, avoidance or panic before tests, a person is likely to help more, and a tutor who can talk to the school may be what makes the difference. Children who find typing or explaining aloud hard, or who have a learning difficulty that needs specialist support, are usually better served by a human first.

  • Does your child do work only when someone is in the room?
  • Do they hide wrong answers instead of asking about them?
  • Is the difficulty emotional as much as academic?
  • Would they type a full explanation, or give up after one line?

Several ticks suggest starting with a person. The article on what works when a child falls behind in Cambridge Maths covers the signs in more depth.

How long should you try a mix before judging it?

Give it one school term, and judge it on more than marks. Marks are noisy over a few weeks, and a Checkpoint-style paper taken once says little about whether a habit has formed. Better signals are whether your child starts sessions without being chased, whether they can explain a topic to you in their own words, and whether the questions they bring to a tutor have become sharper. If none of those has moved after a term, change something: the tutor, the schedule or the tool.

Who should skip Coach?

Skip it if a tutor you trust already marks and explains in the way you need, if your child is not in Stage 9 (Stage 7 and Stage 8 files are planned but not here yet), if you have no reliable internet, or if you do not want to run a Claude account as the adult. Anthropic requires account holders to be at least 18, so there is no version where a child signs up alone. And skip it if the budget is better spent on printed practice, which starts at ₹249 a topic. The Coach page says plainly what is and is not included.

Verdict

Neither is a replacement for the other. Use a person for motivation and judgement, an AI tutor for volume and availability, and decide the mix from what your child is actually struggling with.
