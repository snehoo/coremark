Teach-back means explaining a topic in your own words

Teach-back is a study method in which you explain a topic in your own words, without your notes, and then check the explanation against the source. It is a widely used study technique. The research behind related methods is real but limited, so treat it as a sensible habit with no guarantee of results.

The difference from re-reading is where the effort goes. Re-reading asks your eyes to recognise words you have already seen. Teach-back asks your memory to produce the ideas, and that is where the gaps show up. CoreMark Coach, the Cambridge Lower Secondary Stage 9 tutor that runs inside Claude by Anthropic, uses this method in its Learn mode. The rest of this post works through one real example, then shows how to run the same method at the dinner table with no AI at all.

A real teach-back, read line by line

This excerpt is from a demo session shown on the Coach page, in the Stage 9 Science topic on the greenhouse effect. The student's mistakes are left in, because they are the useful part to study.

Excerpt from a demo session shown on the Coach page (Stage 9 Science, greenhouse effect)

Coach

Your turn — teach it back to me:

What is the difference between the natural greenhouse effect and the enhanced greenhouse effect? 😊

Student

Greenhouse effect is like when if you burn fossil fuel oxygen releases, so the Earth gets more heated, and the temperature rises and effect automatically will happen like it will get a lot of flood. It will affect the marine life. The ice will melt.

Coach

Really good — you’ve nailed the effects of climate change perfectly! 🎯

But there’s one thing to fix:

Burning fossil fuels releases CO₂ — not oxygen!

Oxygen is what we breathe — CO₂ is the greenhouse gas that traps heat.

And you didn’t quite answer the difference between natural vs enhanced greenhouse effect. Here it is:

Natural greenhouse effect = CO₂ and other gases naturally trap some heat → keeps Earth warm enough for life ✅ — this is GOOD

Enhanced greenhouse effect = humans add EXTRA CO₂ (burning fossil fuels, deforestation) → too much heat trapped → Earth gets too warm → climate change ❌ — this is BAD

Key sentence to remember for your exam:

“Human activities increase CO₂ levels, enhancing the greenhouse effect and causing global temperatures to rise.”

Read the student's answer as four jobs. Coach has to say what is right, catch what is wrong, notice what was left out, and give the student one sentence to keep. The table shows how the reply handled each job.

JobWhat the student wroteWhat Coach did
Got rightThe effects: flooding, harm to marine life, melting icePraised the effects specifically, in the first sentence of the reply
Got wrongSays burning fossil fuels releases oxygenCorrected it: burning fossil fuels releases CO₂, not oxygen, and oxygen is what we breathe
Left outNever answered the question about the natural versus enhanced greenhouse effectAnswered it directly: the natural effect keeps Earth warm enough for life, while the enhanced effect comes from extra CO₂ and makes Earth too warm
Something to keepNothing yetAn exam-ready sentence: "Human activities increase CO₂ levels, enhancing the greenhouse effect and causing global temperatures to rise."

The order matters. The praise came first and was specific. The fix for the error took one sentence. The missing part got the longest answer, because the student had not yet learned it. A parent can run the same check on any answer: which parts are right, which are wrong, and which part of the question never got an answer.

Why explaining finds gaps that re-reading hides

Re-reading feels productive because the page looks familiar, and familiar is easy to mistake for understood. A teach-back stalls at the exact point where the idea is fuzzy, so the gap shows up in front of you.

The research points the same way, with limits. A 2013 review by Dunlosky, Rawson, Marsh, Nathan and Willingham looked at ten study techniques, rated by US cognitive psychologists. It rated practice testing and distributed (spaced) practice as high utility, self-explanation and elaborative interrogation as moderate, and rereading as low. The review says the evidence varies by learner, material and task. (Dunlosky et al. (2013), Psychological Science in the Public Interest)

Roediger and Karpicke's 2006 experiments used prose passages. Students who took recall tests remembered substantially more a week later than students who only restudied, even though the restudy group felt more confident. On a test five minutes later, restudying did better. That was a lab study with reading passages, so it does not tell you what will happen with your child's Science notes. (Roediger and Karpicke (2006), Psychological Science)

Cepeda and colleagues combined 839 assessments from 317 experiments in 2006. They found that the best gap between study sessions grows as the material has to be remembered for longer. That review covered verbal recall tasks, so it is about spacing practice out. (Cepeda et al. (2006), Psychological Bulletin)

These are research on testing, spacing and self-explanation, not on teach-back as such. Teach-back is a widely used study technique, and this is the closest research we can point to, so it is a sensible habit, not a settled finding. If your child can follow steps but cannot say why they work, the same gap appears in algebra, and this post on algebra steps and understanding covers it.

Six things to say at the dinner table, with no AI

You do not need a tutor to run a teach-back. Pick one topic from this week's homework, then say these six prompts in order. Let your child answer each one before you add anything.

  1. "Teach it to me like I'm in Stage 7." Your child explains the topic to you as if you were a younger student. Listen for the words they use and the ones they avoid.
  2. "Give me an example from football." Any example from their world will do: games, phones, food or money. If they cannot find one, the idea is probably still fuzzy.
  3. "Say it again without using the key word." This checks whether they understand the idea or only remember the label.
  4. "What do people usually get wrong about this?" Naming a common mistake shows whether they know where the idea stops applying.
  5. "Which bit did you have to guess at?" Children often know where the gap is if you ask directly. Note the answer and return to it in a later session.
  6. "Ask me a question you think I would get wrong." Reversing roles tests whether they can build a question from the idea, not just repeat it.

If your child stalls, ask for the smallest true sentence they can say, then build from there. Giving the full answer straight away skips the part where the gap shows up, so hold back until they have tried.

What a good teach-back sounds like next to a recited definition

A recited definition can be word-perfect and still tell you very little. The table puts two answers to the same question side by side: what the greenhouse effect is. Both are illustrations we wrote, not answers from real students.

"The greenhouse effect is when greenhouse gases trap heat in the atmosphere.""Sunlight warms the ground, and some of that heat tries to escape back into space. CO₂ and a few other gases slow the escape, which is why the planet stays warm enough for life."

The right-hand column is not longer for its own sake. It has an example, a cause and a limit. If your child's answer has none of the three, ask for one. For science vocabulary specifically, this guide on revising science definitions goes into more detail.
