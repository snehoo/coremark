What is an AI tutor for Cambridge Lower Secondary, and what does it do differently?

A worksheet cannot answer "but why?". A chatbot answers everything but follows no plan. An AI tutor sits between the two, and this is what that looks like in practice.

Quick answer

An AI tutor is software that teaches and tests a student through conversation instead of only answering questions. For Cambridge Lower Secondary, CoreMark Coach is one example: a file you add to a Claude Project that teaches Stage 9 Maths, Science and Computing topics in Learn mode, then sets and marks exam-style papers in Exam mode.

CoreMark makes CoreMark Coach, the example used throughout. This page explains the category, and we say where Coach is limited.

Parents keep asking the same thing in different words: is an AI tutor just ChatGPT with a school logo, or is it something else? The honest answer is that the underlying technology is similar and the difference lies in what wraps around it. Below, we define the term, place it next to the other things a family might use, and walk through what one actually does.

What is an AI tutor, in one paragraph?

AI tutor

A program that uses a large language model to teach a topic, ask the student questions, check the answers and decide what to do next, rather than waiting for the student to ask. The word that matters is tutor: it works to a plan, where a general chatbot answers whatever it is asked.

A good one explains in small steps, asks the child to show their own thinking, corrects mistakes kindly and keeps track of what needs another look. A weak one is a chatbot with a friendly name. The test is whether it ever leads the session or only responds.

What does Learn mode do that a worksheet cannot?

Learn mode teaches one idea at a time and asks your child to explain it back. Coach opens with a map of the topic, shows a diagram when one helps, gives an everyday example from your child's world, states the rule in the wording examiners expect, and names the mistake most students make with it. Then it says "teach it back to me".

That last step matters most. If the explanation is vague or recited, Coach is designed to teach the idea again with a different example instead of repeating itself louder. A topic ends with a three-question check that carries no marks. The method behind it is explained in teach-it-back.

What does Exam mode do?

Exam mode sets exam-style practice and marks it afterwards. The sizes are a quick topic test, a longer practice paper or a full Checkpoint-style mock. Questions arrive one at a time with their marks shown, and a mock gives no hints. Only after your child submits everything does Coach mark, showing the worked solution, where method marks and accuracy marks were earned, and the first step that went wrong.

The report is grouped by idea rather than question number, so you see "co-interior angles confused with alternate angles" instead of a list of crosses. The full walk-through is in how CoreMark Coach works.

How can you tell a real AI tutor from a chatbot with a friendly name?

Use five tests, and apply them to any product, ours included. Each one can be checked in a short trial.

  1. It leads. After a topic, it proposes what comes next instead of waiting for a question.
  2. It asks your child to explain. If the student never has to put the idea into their own words, it is a lecture.
  3. It marks working, not just answers. Cambridge awards marks for method, so a tutor that only says right or wrong is missing the point.
  4. It stays in scope. A Stage 9 tutor should say when something belongs to a later qualification.
  5. It admits mistakes. Ask it to show its working on an answer you suspect is wrong. A trustworthy tool re-checks and says so when it slipped.

A sixth, softer test is whether you can read what happened. In Coach's case the chats sit in the parent's own Claude account, so you can look back through them.

Where does an AI tutor fit in Checkpoint preparation?

An AI tutor fits between lessons and exams, in the gap where a child meets a problem at home and nobody is free to explain. Stage 9 is the year most Cambridge schools prepare students for Checkpoint, and your child's school can tell you which tests are taken and when. Practice from any source, Coach included, is practice: it does not predict a Checkpoint band, because Checkpoint results are scaled by Cambridge from the real papers.

What a tutor can do is shorten the loop between getting something wrong and understanding why. That is a smaller promise than "better marks", and a more honest one. If you want the paper-based side of preparation, where to find past papers explains what is and is not publicly available.

What an AI tutor is not

An AI tutor is not a person and not a guarantee of marks, and CoreMark Coach is independent practice material that is not affiliated with Cambridge. Coach practice papers give raw marks, which are not a Checkpoint score; a real result is a 0 to 50 scaled score in six bands, explained in the Checkpoint grading guide. It can be wrong, which is why Coach is built to re-derive answers before marking a student wrong, and why we still suggest you check a few against a worked solution.

What does an AI tutor cost, and what else do you pay for?

Costs fall into three groups. General chatbots have free plans and paid ones, and you supply the tutoring instructions. Purpose-built tutors charge for the structure they add. Printed practice sits at the low end, and a free CoreMark booster lets you judge the style before you spend anything.

For CoreMark Coach, as of 20 October 2026, Stage 9 is ₹9,999 for the school year, paid once, with an early-bird price of ₹4,999 for the first 100 families. Coach is not free and there is no subscription. It runs inside a Claude account, which has its own plans; Anthropic's pricing page lists them, and the free plan has session usage limits that reset every five hours, so long mock papers may call for a paid plan.

Who is an AI tutor for, and who can skip it?

An AI tutor suits a Stage 9 student who understands the lesson in class but cannot explain it, or who gets answers wrong without knowing why. It also suits a parent who cannot sit and re-teach Science every evening. You can skip it if a tutor you trust already covers the same ground, if your child is not in Stage 9 yet, or if you prefer paper. The booster or Coach decision guide lays this out by situation.
